# Narration scripts

Generated by `node tools/narration.mjs` from the running stories: never edit by hand, run it again after a story's
text changes. For each story, the words on its panel chapter by chapter, with the time each line appears (minutes and
seconds on the recording's clock, from NARRATION_SYNC; for a story not recorded yet, that is the pace `tools/pace.mjs`
set from these words). A narration follows these words and times; it can say them more freely, but the numbers must
stay as they are (they come from the data).

How to record and fit a narration: see "Recording a narration" in `audio/PUT-narration.mp3-HERE.txt`. In short, put the
MP3 at the file named under each story; if your chapters start at other times, change NARRATION_SYNC (pairs of
[time in the recording, story time]) in the story's source (`tools/<story>/engine/e3_story.js`, then rebuild; in
`stories/<story>.js` for the airports, proteins and spatial stories) and run the smoke test.

### 0:28 Scanner

- **0:28** How PET sees
- **0:30** A PET scan, short for positron emission tomography, starts with a tracer: a molecule carrying a radioactive atom. The most common, and the one in this story, is FDG, a glucose look-alike carrying fluorine-18.
- **0:45** After at least 6 hours without food, it goes into a vein. For about 60 minutes the person rests: cells take it up like glucose, and an enzyme, hexokinase, traps it inside.
- **0:58** Then the scan. Around the tunnel sits a ring of detectors.
- **1:03** A positron: fluorine-18 decays and sends one out, the electron's antimatter twin; it travels about a millimetre, drawn larger here
- **1:11** Two photons, one line: it meets an electron and both vanish: two gamma photons of 511 keV fly off in opposite directions. Two detectors on opposite sides of the ring register both within a few billionths of a second, and the line between them passes close to where the tracer is
- **1:33** The uptake is measured as the SUV, the standardised uptake value: the tracer in a tissue divided by the dose per kilogram of body weight, so 1 would be an even spread. Protocol as in the paper: 250 to 300 MBq of FDG.

### 1:50 Sugar

- **1:51** Where the sugar goes
- **1:57** The tracer arrives with the blood, and within the hour it settles where cells use sugar.
- **2:07** The brain takes up the most, and the heart often does too, though this varies greatly from person to person. The kidneys and the bladder glow too: FDG is filtered into the urine and mostly not taken back, so it leaves the body that way.
- **2:26** A tumour shows as a hot spot. This is the main application of PET scans in routine care: finding and staging cancer.
- **2:36** Yet the scan holds every organ's metabolism at once.

- **3:07** Brain: mean of the whole brain: it lives on sugar
- **3:11** Liver: mean of the whole liver
- **3:15** Muscle: mean of the gluteus maximus (the buttock muscle)
- **3:20** One scan, one row of numbers.
- **3:23** The organs' values shown here are typical uptakes, for illustration.

### 3:27 Network

- **3:28** 658 scans
- **3:28** A network like this needs many scans: one scan gives only one row of numbers. These networks are the paper's, each of a group of scans.

- **0:04** The ovaries hold the eggs, each in a small sac of cells: a follicle.
- **0:10** Every ovary starts with a reserve of follicles that is never refilled.

## Quantum network compression: Three qubits (`#qubits`)

File: `audio/narration-qubits.mp3` (not recorded yet). Chapters: 0:00 Pairs, 0:51 Walkers, 1:50 Shrink, 2:32 Cuts, 4:02 Coarse, 5:02 Bricks, 6:32 Toolbox, 7:53 Circuit; it ends at 9:04.

### 0:00 Pairs

- **0:01** Three qubits
- **0:02** how a gene network fits on a tiny quantum computer
- **0:07** Some diseases only appear when two genes are broken at the same time.
- **0:12** The dots on the diagonal are genes, and every dot above them a pair. Add genes, and the pairs grow much faster.
- **0:22** 16,064: genes in the real network
- **0:24** 129 million: pairs of them
- **0:27** Far too many to test one by one.
- **0:33** n genes make n(n − 1)/2 pairs: 16,064 × 16,063 / 2 = 129,018,016. The real network: STRING v12.5, physical protein interactions with a combined score of 500 or more, its largest connected component.
- **0:44** After the method and figures of Joel Hancock. The networks drawn are examples, computed here.

### 0:51 Walkers

- **0:51** Two walkers on a map
- **0:53** Think of the network as a map. Each gene is a place, and a street joins two genes whose proteins touch.
- **1:03** Two walkers wander it, one for each gene of a pair.
- **1:13** They are drawn to pairs that are broken together in patients more often than in healthy people.
- **1:20** Where they settle is where to look.
- **1:24** In symbols, the walkers' energy is H = K + c V + ρ R. K = I − A_c lets them move along the network (A_c comes later); V rewards the pairs that patients' data point to; R, the exchange operator, swaps the two walkers, so the search is for pairs, not single genes.
- **1:45** The valleys here are illustrative: this story uses no patients' data.

### 1:50 Shrink

- **1:50** Why shrink the map?
- **1:52** A quantum computer could explore all the walkers' routes at once. But today's machines are tiny.
- **1:59** 3 qubits can only tell 8 places apart. So the map has to be shrunk first.
- **2:05** It is folded into 8 neighborhoods, and each gets a 3-digit code, like a postal code: 000 to 111.
- **2:14** Each digit is one qubit: q2, q1 and q0, read left to right. A bright qubit is a 1.
- **2:22** A qubit is a quantum bit. L qubits address 2^L places, one per basis state: 3 qubits give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Each walker needs its own 3 qubits.

### 2:32 Cuts

- **2:33** Cutting the map
- **2:34** Here is an example network, made up for this story: 320 genes and 5,637 links, with eight groups hidden in it. Its genes are never named.
- **2:45** 1 · First cut: split the genes in two, keeping linked genes together: two halves, one digit
- **2:56** 2 · Second cut: split each half again: four cells, two digits (a square)
- **3:07** 3 · Third cut: and again: eight cells, three digits (a cube)
- **3:13** A gene's code is the side it fell on at each cut. Genes that share a code form a cell, at one corner of the cube.
- **3:25** Each cut is a bisection. The first is the most significant digit (q2), so cells nest: each new cut splits every cell in two. The real run used 8 cuts: 256 cells of about 63 genes; the picture uses 3.
- **3:41** The cuts here stand in for the authors' search (a beam search that also favors the toolbox of chapter 7): the network's slowest patterns grouped into eight equal cells, then the codes that keep the most links on the cube's edges. It puts 100% of genes with their hidden group.

### 4:02 Coarse

- **4:02** The coarse operator
- **4:04** Now each corner is a cell, and the shrunk map says how much traffic flows between cells: the coarse operator, A_c.
- **4:13** A loop at each corner is the traffic that stays inside its cell. An edge joins every two corners, as wide as the traffic between them.
- **4:24** Cube edges join cells one digit apart. Here they carry 79% of the traffic between cells; face and body diagonals, two or three digits apart, the rest.
- **4:36** A_c = P A Pᵀ: A is the network's adjacency matrix, and P the normalized cell indicators, so A_c is an 8 × 8 table. It is balanced over cells (Sinkhorn), so every row adds up to 1. The walkers move by K = I − A_c: what holds for A_c holds for K.
- **4:56** In the real network, at 3 bits, cube edges carry 61% of the coupling between cells.

### 5:02 Bricks

- **5:03** Building blocks
- **5:04** Any traffic table can be built from a few kinds of simple moves: Lego bricks of movement, called Pauli strings.
- **5:13** I: do nothing: the same loop on every corner
- **5:16** X₀, X₁, X₂flip one digit: hops along the cube’s edges in one direction
- **5:26** Z₂a sign by digit: loops of +1 (solid, red) and −1 (dashed, blue)
- **5:31** Z₂X₁hops with signs
- **5:34** X₂X₁, Y₂Y₁flip two digits at once: face diagonals (Y with signs)
- **5:41** Any traffic table is a recipe of these bricks, the way a sound is a mix of pure tones.
- **5:49** Here they are added largest first, and the rebuilt cube comes close to the true one: after ten bricks the error left is 0.07, down from 1.
- **6:00** A Pauli string puts one Pauli matrix on each qubit: I (do nothing), X (flip the bit), Z (+1 on 0, −1 on 1) or Y (flip with a sign). Subscripts are qubits; q2 is the first cut. Any 8 × 8 table is one weighted sum of the 64 strings. Here: I 0.55, X₀ 0.17, X₁ 0.11, X₂ 0.08, X₁X₀ 0.04 …
- **6:22** In the real network the largest weights are I 0.40, X₁ 0.20, X₂X₁ 0.13, X₂ 0.11, Z₂Z₁ 0.08, Y₂Y₁ 0.07, X₀ 0.06, Z₀ 0.05, X₁X₀ 0.04, and a tail.

### 6:32 Toolbox

- **6:32** The toolbox
- **6:33** A quantum computer runs some moves quickly and others hardly at all.
- **6:38** Keep only the bricks in its quick toolbox, a Lie algebra; the rest goes gray and is dropped.
- **6:46** What is dropped is the residual: 0 means nothing lost, 1 everything. Here it is 0.34 with three qubits.
- **6:53** The toolbox: the TFIM chain's moves (X on each qubit, ZZ on neighbors) close to so(6), 15 strings at 3 qubits; Z on q2 as well gives so(7), 21 strings. Closed means the commutators of its strings stay inside, so its operators run as short circuits. Residual = ‖A₀ − ΠA₀‖ / ‖A₀‖, with A₀ the traceless part and Π the projection onto the algebra.
- **7:19** In the real network, every extra bit makes the fit worse.
- **7:24** Our cuts lose 0.44 at 2 bits, 0.52 at 3 and 0.56 at 4, where random cuts lose 0.57, 0.75 and 0.86. At 8 bits ours lose 0.88 (the best variant 0.82): most of the operator falls outside the toolbox.
- **7:36** The example network, computed here: 0.00 at 1 bit, 0.12 at 2 and 0.34 at 3 (random cuts: 0.56 at 2, 0.75 at 3).
- **7:43** At 1 bit every cut loses nothing. In the real network at 3 bits, so(7)'s generators carry 66% of A_c's traceless weight, and the whole algebra 73%.

### 7:53 Circuit

- **7:53** The recipe as a circuit
- **7:56** What is kept runs as a short circuit: six gates on three qubits, each a small turn.
- **8:03** Red gates flip (X), blue gates add signs (Z); the deeper the color, the larger the turn.
- **8:10** One Trotter step of e^(−iKt) at 3 bits in so(7), with the real network's angles: RX on q0, q1 and q2 (θ = −0.112, −0.396, −0.222), RZZ on (q2, q1) at −0.163 and (q1, q0) at +0.039, RZ on q2 at +0.029. θ = −2ct, with c = tr(P A_c)/8 and R_P(θ) = exp(−iθP/2), t = 1.
- **8:29** From thousands of genes to 8 cells on 3 qubits, and from a traffic table to a few bricks a quantum computer runs well.
- **8:39** Next: more qubits need larger toolboxes or better cuts, and then the patients' data, V, go in.
- **8:46** Method and figures: Joel Hancock.
- **8:49** The networks shown are examples, computed here; numbers marked "real network" are from the authors' run on STRING v12.5. The cuts here stand in for their beam search, and the valleys are illustrative.

## Quantum phylogenetics: The mutation machine (`#mutation`)

### 0:00 Letters

- **0:01** The mutation machine
- **0:02** DNA's changes as a quantum circuit
- **0:05** DNA is a text written in four letters: A, C, G and T.
- **0:10** Every time it is copied, now and then a letter is swapped for another. That is a mutation.
- **0:18** Here an ancestor's eight letters, ACGTTAGC, are copied twice, and each copy swaps two of them.
- **0:25** Copy 1 · ACGTCGGC: T→C at letter 5 and A→G at letter 6
- **0:31** Copy 2 · ACATTAGT: G→A at letter 3 and C→T at letter 8

### 0:43 Family tree

- **0:44** A family tree
- **0:45** Copied over many generations, one text becomes many: a family tree.
- **0:51** Where two branches part lived a shared ancestor.
- **0:56** Each copy carries the letters up its branch. Where one flashes, a letter was swapped.
- **1:10** A branch's length says how much changed along it: the expected number of swaps per letter, which grows with time.
- **1:19** 0.47: swaps per letter on a long branch: 4 swaps here
- **1:23** 0.15: on a short one: 1 swap
- **1:25** Chance gives more or fewer swaps; the length is what to expect. Trees like this trace viruses as they spread [1] and how a tumour grew from one cancer cell [6], and the rule of change they assume matters [2].
- **1:41** The tree's 14 branches and their swaps are examples, simulated with the story's rule. Heights are change: each branch rises by its length.

### 1:50 Qubits

- **1:51** Letters as qubits
- **1:52** A quantum computer can hold each letter in two qubits, drawn as arrows on spheres: up is 0, down is 1.
- **2:00** A is |00⟩first up, second up
- **2:03** C is |01⟩first up, second down
- **2:07** G is |10⟩first down, second up
- **2:10** T is |11⟩first down, second down
- **2:14** Tilt the arrows and the two qubits hold a mix of all four letters at once, like a spinning coin.
- **2:23** Reading them out makes the coin land on one letter, each with its own chance.
- **2:29** Here the chances are A 71 %, C 5 %, G 19 % and T 6 %; 24 read-outs gave 18 A, 1 C, 4 G and 1 T.
- **2:36** |01⟩ means first 0, then 1. An arrow shorter than its sphere means the two qubits depend on each other. This mix is where the rule of the next chapter takes an A after 0.45 swaps per letter.

### 2:50 The rule

- **2:51** The rule
- **2:52** A substitution model is the rule for how letters change:
- **2:56** Thicker arrows: how readily one letter swaps for another
- **2:59** Bigger balls: how common each letter is in the long run
- **3:04** Here A↔G and C↔T swap about four times as readily as the other pairs, and A is the most common letter in the long run (32 %).
- **3:14** Every mix of the four letters is a point in this pyramid, the pure letters at its corners.
- **3:22** Start at A, and as time goes on the rule moves the mix along a path towards the long-run mix.
- **3:31** After 0.15, 0.45 and 1 swaps per letter it has come 17 %, 41 % and 65 % of the way; after 1.6, 78 %.
- **3:36** The most general standard rule is GTR, the general time-reversible model. The mix follows p(t) = p(0) e^(Qt), a dynamical system: Q holds the rates. The rates and shares here are examples.

### 3:49 Machine

- **3:50** The machine
- **3:51** A compiler turns the rule into a quantum circuit: a machine of gears for one branch.
- **3:58** 1 · Copy: the parent's letter is stamped onto the child
- **4:04** 2 · Turn: the child's qubits turn: the longer the branch, the further, and the child's mix lands where the rule's path is at that time
- **4:14** 3 · The circuit: four wires, the parent's two qubits and the child's, with two copy gates and two turn blocks
- **4:21** One machine per branch, joined into the tree.
- **4:29** Read at the tips, it writes new sequences that follow the rule.
- **4:35** The copy is two CNOT gates. The turn is a few rotations of single qubits plus steps that link two qubits, so it depends on the parent's letter: for a G after 1 swap per letter, c1, the child's first qubit, turns 84° and c0, its second, 55° or 56°. How far each step turns depends only on the branch length; the steps are the same on every branch: 12 turns of one qubit and 12 links.

### 5:05 Pairs

- **5:05** Letters that work together
- **5:07** Letters often act together.
- **5:10** An RNA strand folds back on itself into a stem, held by pairs: A with T, C with G, G with T.
- **5:21** If one letter changes alone, its pair breaks.
- **5:29** Coupling lets the two letters of a pair change together, so the pair holds.
- **5:36** Models of pairs exist, but each linked letter multiplies the combinations by four.
- **5:42** 16: combinations for a pair
- **5:46** 1,048,576: for ten letters: about a million
- **5:49** 20: qubits for ten: two per letter
- **5:51** T stands for RNA's U here, as on the poster. On every branch the coupling acts on both letters of a pair beside the turn: changes that keep a pair are more likely (eight times, in the authors' example), and both letters may change at once [3].

### 6:11 Rebuild

- **6:12** Rebuilding the ancestor
- **6:13** Three species carry the same stem, each changed in its own way.
- **6:19** What did their shared ancestor have?
- **6:27** Rebuild it 2,000 times with each method, and count the ancestors whose six pairs all hold:
- **6:33** Letter by letter: 10 % of them
- **6:37** The exact pair calculation: 20 %
- **6:40** With the coupled circuit: 43 %
- **6:43** So the coupling favours pairs too strongly: its ancestors look believable, but its percentages are not true chances.
- **6:51** And a broken pair is normal: the true ancestor here has one, and only about a quarter of ancestors have all six pairs matched.

### 7:26 What for

- **7:27** What it is for
- **7:28** Rebuilding ancestors is how researchers brought back old virus proteins for vaccine research [4, 5] and trace how a tumour grew from one cancer cell [6].

### 0:24 Seven

- **0:26** No bird leads. Each one keeps track of about seven of its nearest neighbours, however near or far they are, and matches their heading and speed.

- **1:23** Because every bird is linked to its neighbours, a turn can spread across the whole flock, however big it is.
